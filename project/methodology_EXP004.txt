EXP-004: weak-interaction phase sparsification
Internal research explanation, not final poster prose or a poster caption.

Question
  Can a smaller QAOA phase circuit retain essentially the same average decoding
  quality on the 64 toy Module-LWE development examples?

Mechanism
  Our binary decoding cost becomes an Ising expression with single-qubit fields
  h_i and pair interactions J_ij. Each nonzero pair interaction contributes a
  two-qubit ZZ rotation to the phase circuit. In the chosen CX gate basis, a
  ZZ rotation normally requires two CX gates. Removing weak interactions can
  therefore reduce circuit cost, but changes the sampling distribution.
  For example, if four interaction magnitudes are 0.01, 0.04, 0.3, and 0.8,
  removing the weakest 25% deletes the 0.01 interaction from the circuit only.
  Every returned candidate is still scored using all four original terms.
  A small coefficient alone does not guarantee a small effect on success.

Prior work and scope of the adaptation
  Liu, Shaydulin, Safro, Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm with
  Sparsified Phase Operator (IEEE QCE 2022):
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00118
  https://doi.org/10.1109/QCE53715.2022.00032
  This establishes sparse phase operators with full-objective optimization on
  MaxCut. Our magnitude rule is a simple adaptation, not their cut-based rule
  and not a new general QAOA algorithm. No novelty or preservation theorem is
  claimed. Ground-state agreement is a diagnostic, not a solver input.

Protocol fixed before this run
  Primary: remove the smallest-magnitude 25% of nonzero J entries and reuse the
  previous frozen global angle pair. Keep fields, normalization, encoding,
  mixer, initial state, number of qubits, and scored objective unchanged.
  Success targets: at least 95% aggregate single-shot optimum probability,
  at least 95% aggregate solved fraction at 1024 shots, and at least 20% fewer
  mean compiled CX gates. All three targets passed on this development cohort.
  Secondary: 10% and 50% removal; global retraining at each level; original full
  per-instance tuned QAOA; five random 25% removals per example. Strong classical
  controls from EXP-003 remain available and still perform better overall.
  Retraining uses 16 separate public training examples and minimizes expected
  ORIGINAL energy. No hidden secrets or optimum labels enter method selection.
  These 64 evaluation examples were previously inspected; this is exploration,
  not a fresh final holdout or a confirmatory statistical claim.

Verified results: exact ideal probabilities, same 1024-shot budget per example
  Method                  Mean compiled CX     Expected fraction solved
  Full fixed QAOA             202.875                   22.3460%
  Full per-instance tuned     202.875                   22.4338%
  Weak 10%, frozen            183.5625                  22.4191%
  Weak 25%, frozen            152.15625                 22.5273%
  Weak 50%, frozen            101.4375                  23.6830%
  Random 25%, frozen        same logical removal       14.6300%
  Random control averages five independent masks per example; not retrained.

  Expected solved fraction is mean_i[1-(1-p_i)^1024], not a calculation using
  the mean probability. An optimum means the global CVP optimum of our stated
  cost, not necessarily the planted secret. All 64 binary boxes cover one.
  Weak 25% retains 101.30% of mean single-shot optimum probability; the paired
  instance-bootstrap 95% interval is [98.50%,104.11%]. This supports preserved
  average quality, not a robust claim of improved quality.
  At 25% removal, mean circuit depth decreases 8.60%, not 25%. Gate count is
  distinct from depth, physical duration, and IBM charged execution time.
  No additional angle-training cost is incurred by the primary frozen method.

What the average conceals
  18/64 examples lose more than 5% of single-shot optimum probability; the worst
  loses 31.29%. Only 33/64 sparse phase ground-state sets overlap an original
  optimum. This is an approximate circuit change, not exact gate cancellation.
  Keeping the Babai incumbent, expected hybrid success slightly decreases from
  86.9070% to 86.8270%. It is not a demonstrated improvement in hybrid decoding.
  Half-removal is a promising secondary result but has a worst loss of 41.58%.
  We have not established performance at deeper QAOA, other parameter sizes,
  actual device connectivity, physical noise, or secure cryptographic sizes.

Why investigate QAOA for lattice decoding?
  A bounded lattice-decoding task naturally gives a quadratic binary cost.
  QAOA uses that cost to bias quantum samples toward better candidates. The
  testable question is whether that search mechanism can become effective and
  resource-efficient; the mapping itself does not imply a quantum speedup.
  Priestley and Wallden study reusable angles and empirical refinement scaling
  on prime lattices: https://doi.org/10.1088/2058-9565/ae4cc6
  Their instance family, conditional refinement metric, and scaling comparisons
  differ from ours; their results do not establish an ML-KEM attack or an
  advantage over our strong classical baselines.
  Lv et al. study QAOA refinement of Babai for LWE:
  https://doi.org/10.3390/e24101428
  Their section 5.1 explicitly omits actual QAOA complexity. Better distance in
  small tests therefore does not establish a faster complete decoding method.
  Our defensible aim is to measure and improve the resource/quality trade-off
  of this candidate quantum heuristic. An open reproducible implementation can
  help others test the same trade-offs, including failures.

Next evidence required
  Freeze the 25% primary rule and evaluate fresh lattice groups. Treat 50% as a
  secondary option. Examine failures and deeper circuits on development data,
  then compile for a specific backend and model noise and resource usage before
  a small budgeted hardware comparison. Real hardware can test whether ideal
  savings survive routing and noise; that benefit is not yet demonstrated.
